# Point of View — What I Would Actually Do

*The recommendation layer. Everything else in this pack is evidence; this page is the position. First person, deliberately opinionated — the analysis says "it depends," this page says "I'd bet."*

---

## The position in one sentence

Everyone will have the same models, so I would spend the next twelve months building the three things that can't be bought later — a credible trust bargain with our people, a paved road through governance, and proof from two end-to-end rebuilds — and I would fund them by stopping things, not by adding budget.

---

## Three moves, in order

**Move 1 (months 0–6): Fix the preconditions — the trust bargain and the paved road.**
Before any further rollout: a public, specific commitment that AI productivity gains flow to growth and redeployment first, with redundancy as a last resort — co-designed with the FSU, not announced at them. In parallel, build the paved road: pre-approved use-case patterns by risk tier, an evaluation harness with promotion gates (assist → recommend → act-with-review), and a target of days-not-months approval for standard use cases. Nothing else in this strategy compounds until people surface what they learn and teams can ship without a bespoke committee. This move is cheap, fast, and almost nobody does it first — which is exactly why it differentiates.

**Move 2 (months 3–12): Rebuild two things end-to-end and retire what they replace.**
Not fifty pilots — two full rebuilds chosen for evidence value: one customer journey where the human stakes are highest (complaints or hardship — agent-assist first, closed-loop learning built in), and one finance process where the assurance stakes are highest (the close, or management reporting — continuous reconciliation, exception-based control). Success is defined as: the legacy process is switched off, and the external auditor accepts the new one. These two become the internal proof that redesign beats retrofit — the demonstration assets every later conversation points at.

**Move 3 (months 6–12): Stand up the diffusion machinery — and publish the stop-list the same day.**
A small floating expert bench (elite, venture-style, measured on capability left behind), a pattern library that turns the two rebuilds into reusable assets, and metabolism metrics on the exec scorecard — weekly active AI use by role, time from validated pilot to third business-unit adoption, and the gap between our top and bottom quartile of AI fluency. Announced alongside a stop-list of programs we are cancelling or re-scoping because AI has invalidated their design. The stop-list is what makes the strategy credible; without it, this is a wish.

## What I would explicitly not do

1. **No enterprise-wide copilot rollout ahead of Moves 1 and 3.** Licences before trust and measurement is how you buy the 84%-implemented / 7%-impact outcome at scale.
2. **No innovation lab, and no central AI factory that owns use cases.** Central guardrails, federated adoption. A lab is where metabolism goes to be admired instead of spread.
3. **No heavy engineering around current model weaknesses.** Anything that exists to compensate for today's context window, reasoning, or autonomy limits gets bought thin or not built — agent capability is doubling every few months; scaffolding is depreciating while we write the business case.
4. **No announced headcount benefits ahead of evidence.** CBA paid for that lesson in 2025; we get it free.

## The arithmetic (my numbers, deliberately rough)

Take a big-four cost base of ~$11B, of which roughly 60% is people — call it $6.5B. Bain's sector data says ~20% productivity gains in exposed functions are already the *baseline*; assume we convert only half of that, on only half the workforce, into net capacity over five years. That is still ~$650M a year of run-rate capacity — redeployable to growth, risk reduction, or cost. **The cost of an 18-month adoption lag is therefore roughly $1B cumulative — before counting the revenue side, and before counting the compounding capability gap that a lag creates.** The precision is fake; the order of magnitude is not. That's the number that makes sequencing an executive issue rather than a technology one.

## The bear case, steelmanned — and my kill criteria

The credible skeptic says: productivity technologies take decades to show up in aggregate numbers (the Solow paradox); the AI capex cycle looks bubble-shaped; and whatever gains materialise will be competed away to customers, as digital's were. I take all three seriously — and note that the 95%-of-pilots-fail statistic *supports* my position, not the skeptic's: value hasn't shown up broadly because most organisations bolt AI onto old processes, which is precisely the thing this strategy refuses to do.

But I'll state what would prove me wrong:

- **If by mid-2027 our top-quartile AI users show no measurable outcome advantage** over the bottom quartile on cycle time, quality, or rework — the metabolism thesis is wrong for us, and this is a tooling story after all.
- **If the paved road doesn't cut approval time for standard use cases by ~80% within 12 months** — then governance-as-accelerator is a slogan, and the constraint is somewhere I haven't diagnosed.
- **If the two end-to-end rebuilds deliver no more value than the equivalent spend on point automations** — then redesign-over-retrofit is wrong, and the fast-follower strategy dominates.

I'd rather run a strategy with published kill criteria than one that can't fail.

## In the room — the kit

- **Three numbers:** 95/5 (MIT — pilots without metabolism fail) · 10-20-70 (BCG — the effort is people and process) · #4 (Evident — an Australian bank is already a global AI leader; ambition is not the constraint).
- **Three questions, matched to the audience:** CRO — *could our governance let us say yes faster and safer than our competitors?* · CFO — *our auditor will soon test 100% of our journals with AI; how long can our own controls stay sample-based?* · CEO — *when our customer's AI agent tries to move their money, are we the trust anchor or a bystander?*
- **The close:** *Everyone will have the models. Advantage belongs to the organisation that metabolises them fastest — and metabolism is a choice we make in the next twelve months, not a capability we buy later.*
