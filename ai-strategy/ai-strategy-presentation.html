AI  ·  Operating Models  ·  Comparative Advantage

Metabolising AI

Building comparative advantage in a large Australian bank

A strategic conversation about where enterprise AI advantage will actually come from — and how an organisation absorbs new ways of thinking, not just new tools.

The core thesis

Everyone will have the models.
Advantage belongs to whoever metabolises them fastest.

Frontier AI is commoditising. Every large bank will buy broadly the same capability at the same time. The differentiator is the operating model: how quickly we absorb the technology, redesign work around it, manage the risk, uplift our people, and diffuse new behaviours across tens of thousands of employees.

How do we turn AI from a set of tools and pilots into an organisational capability — something that changes how risk, finance, customer service, talent, and work itself are designed?
1 · Comparative advantage

Tool access is table stakes. Advantage sits in the connective tissue.

Proprietary data

Valuable only if governed, connected and usable — not trapped in silos.

Workflow integration

AI embedded deep in lending, payments, risk and service flows is slow to copy.

Risk & governance maturity

A regulated licence to move fast is rare and hard-won.

Customer trust

Trust determines what customers will let the bank do with AI.

Speed of adoption

Diffusion speed compounds — a 12-month lead becomes a capability gap.

Redesigning work

Automating tasks yields 10–20%. Redesigning work yields step changes. This is the biggest lever.

2 · Risk & governance

Governance as the accelerator, not the brake

Policy-as-code

Obligations and risk appetite expressed as machine-readable rules, checked in real time.

Embedded controls

Controls executed inside the workflow, not inspected after it.

Auditability by design

Every AI-assisted decision carries its own automatically generated evidence trail.

Real-time assurance

AI tests controls at 100% coverage; humans stop sampling 5%.

Judgement-focused oversight

People handle exceptions, appeals and judgement — not routine checking.

The paved road

Pre-approved patterns and risk tiers so 80% of use cases need configuration, not committee.

What does good look like for AI-enabled risk processes? Could strong governance let us say yes faster — and more safely — than our competitors?

5 · The platform shift

From static artefacts to a live operating layer

  • Live pages, not decks — interactive artefacts connected to real data replace the 40-slide monthly pack.
  • Dashboards wired to workflows — "see the problem" and "fix the problem" become one motion.
  • Copilots embedded in daily work — drawing on organisational knowledge in context.
  • Decisions linked to their artefacts — approvals, commentary and evidence create automatic institutional memory.
  • The substrate for agents — AI agents can act on connected, current information — not on a folder of old PowerPoints.
Discussion

Are we moving away from PowerPoint and email toward a connected platform where knowledge, workflows, dashboards, copilots, approvals and commentary sit in one live operating layer?

6 · The widening skill gap

AI will not lift everyone evenly

Superpowered

Use AI deeply, redesign their own workflows, become dramatically more productive. Risk: critical workflows depend on a handful of people.

New normal

Adopt AI as routine; steady gains. Need a paved road of approved tools and playbooks, not hype.

Laggards

Struggle, quietly resist, or fall behind. Need protected time and peer support — not another e-learning module.

An organisation split into super-users and laggards has an operating-model problem wearing a training costume: concentration risk, fragmentation, broken performance measurement, and growing internal inequality of capability.

Discussion

AI may create super-users, a new normal, and laggards. How do we stop that from becoming an operating-model problem?

8 · Metabolising new ways of thinking

The hardest part isn't the technology — it's digestion

01 · INGEST

Adapt frontier ideas

Make new ways of working safe and usable in a regulated enterprise — adapt, don't copy.

02 · DIGEST

Pilots → patterns

Turn successes into repeatable, governed, reusable patterns — not one-off wins.

03 · CIRCULATE

Move it across units

Rotations, communities of practice, pattern libraries, floating experts.

04 · ABSORB

Make it the default

Built into onboarding, performance expectations and management routines.

The common failure mode: pockets of genuine innovation that never scale, because no mechanism exists to move them. The pilot succeeds; the organisation doesn't change.

Discussion

How do we metabolise AI-native ways of thinking across the enterprise, rather than leaving them trapped in pilots, innovation teams, or isolated power users?

9 · Global comparators

Stop benchmarking against other Australian banks

Europe · Structural comparator

"What must we get right?"

Mature markets, strong regulation (EU AI Act), similar trust expectations. Banks that integrate frontier AI safely rather than build it.

Learn regulated, responsible, enterprise-scale adoption. Avoid compliance timidity.

US East Coast · Aspirational banking

"What does scale look like?"

Large banks, asset managers, insurers industrialising AI: platforms, thousands of production use cases, CEO-level ownership.

Learn industrialisation and talent density. Don't assume their budget is the entry ticket.

US West Coast · Frontier signal

"What's coming?"

Agentic workflows, AI-native platforms, coding agents, copilots as the default interface, radically faster iteration.

Learn the direction of travel. Don't copy the risk posture onto a regulated balance sheet.

Discussion

Europe for regulation, East Coast for enterprise transformation, West Coast for frontier operating models — how do we learn from each without copying the wrong context?

10 · Talent

The floating expert layer: retention and diffusion in one structure

  • A small elite bench — AI, engineering, data and product talent deployed against the highest-priority problems.
  • Venture-style bursts — short, deep problem-solving with real autonomy — the problem quality and velocity that keeps this talent.
  • Patterns, not one-offs — every engagement leaves behind reusable assets and capability, not just a deliverable.
  • Coaching as they deliver — capability transfers to resident teams rather than departing with the experts.
  • The circulatory system — floating experts are how AI-native practices physically move across the organisation.
Discussion

Top tech talent won't stay boxed into narrow BAU roles. Does our operating model need a floating expert layer that solves the hardest problems and spreads new ways of working?

Part II

The harder angles

The questions a sharp room will ask. Not caveats — design problems, each with a better answer than the default the organisation drifts into.

Trust bargain · Junior pipeline · Foundations · Measurement · Timing · Saturation · Organisational shape · Partnerships

Part II · 12 — The internal trust bargain

Diffusion runs on disclosure. Disclosure runs on safety.

  • Secret cyborgs are already here — employees report ~3× more AI use than their C-suite estimates (McKinsey). Gains are real, but private — adoption without metabolism.
  • The local cautionary tale — CBA declared 45 roles redundant citing its voice bot; call volumes rose, the FSU pushed back, and the bank reversed and admitted the error. Every bank employee in Australia updated their answer to "what happens to me if I get faster?"
  • Value-sharing must be credible — gains flow to growth and redeployment first; redundancy is a last resort, not a first announcement.
  • Make disclosure safe and rewarded — amnesty plus a paved road converts shadow AI into organisational capability.
Discussion

If an employee doubles their productivity tomorrow, what do they honestly believe happens to them — and does that belief accelerate or kill diffusion?

Part II · 13 — The junior pipeline paradox

AI does the junior work. Juniors were how we built seniors.

Apprenticeship destroyer

Employment for 22–25-year-old developers is down ~20% since 2024 (Stanford AI Index). Juniors become reviewers of work they never learned to produce — with no basis for knowing when the AI is wrong.

Apprenticeship accelerator

AI encoding expert practice lifted novices +34% (Brynjolfsson) — the fastest learning curve ever built. Which one we get is a design choice.

  • Deliberate practice, not throughput — juniors form a view first, then confront the AI's, with the delta coached.
  • Simulation + exception queues — compress a decade of judgement into safe, high-feedback reps; staff exceptions with junior–senior pairs.
  • Measure time-to-competence — find out in 2027, not 2032. And keep hiring juniors.
Discussion

If AI does the 2026 analyst's work, where does the 2032 senior credit officer come from?

Part II · 14 — Foundations

The unglamorous prerequisites: data, permissions, supply chain

Data

The proprietary-data advantage is only real if data is usable. The richest fuel — call transcripts, credit memos, procedures — is the least governed. Treat knowledge as a tier-1 asset with named owners.

Entitlements & legacy

An AI agent is only as safe as the permission model it runs in. Access designed for humans becomes dangerous at machine speed. Entitlement redesign is on the critical path to the agentic future.

Vendor concentration

APRA has flagged it: dependence on few providers, weak contingency. Model-agnostic abstraction, dual-sourcing critical workloads, rehearsed exit plans.

Sequence by use case — fix data, permissions and integration for the journeys being rebuilt, then expand along the path of value.

Discussion

Which matters more over the next two years — deploying more AI, or making our data, permissions and supply chain AI-ready? Do our funding decisions reflect the answer?

Part II · 15 — Measurement & workslop

Activity metrics lie. Workslop is what they hide.

41%

of US desk workers received AI "workslop" in the past month — polished output that shifts the real work downstream (Stanford / BetterUp).

~2 hrs

to untangle each incident — ~$186 per worker per month; $9M+ a year for a 10,000-person organisation.

7%

of finance organisations report high AI impact, though 84% have implemented it (Gartner). The gap is a measurement gap.

  • Measure outcomes, not activity — cycle time, rework, quality, customer outcomes. Instrument the receiving end of work.
  • Workslop is management-manufactured — it grows where "use AI more" arrives without standards or training. Treat it as a process defect with a defect rate.
Discussion

How do we distinguish genuine AI leverage from polished noise — what is our defect rate for AI-assisted work?

Part II · 16 — Timing under fast capability growth

Be a fast follower on scaffolding. Be early on metabolism.

Durable — build now, compounds

Data quality and products · entitlements · evaluation harnesses · policy-as-code and audit trails · adoption muscle and patterns · redesigned workflows (they get better as models improve).

Perishable — hold lightly, buy

Engineering that compensates for a current model weakness · heavy orchestration built for today's autonomy level · anything vendors are racing to commoditise.

The durable list is the operating model. Scaffolding can be bought later; metabolism only grows with reps — it cannot be bought at any price.

Discussion

Which of our AI investments would survive a model ten times better arriving next year — and which are workarounds we should hold lightly?

Part II · 17 — Change saturation

An AI strategy that only adds is a wish

  • Change capacity is the scarcest resource — frontline attention, engineering hours, risk-review bandwidth. AI layered onto a saturated agenda produces shallow adoption everywhere.
  • Publish a stop-list alongside the start-list — credibility comes from what gets cancelled, descoped or paused to make room.
  • Re-scope in-flight programs against current AI — a standing test: would we start this program today, designed this way?
  • Retire what AI replaces — when the AI-enabled process ships, switch the legacy process off. Running both consumes change capacity twice for one outcome.
  • Measure change load per team — and sequence adoption around it.
Discussion

Which in-flight programs would we not start today, knowing what AI can do — and what will we visibly stop?

Part II · 18 — The shape of the organisation

Layers, spans and team size are the quiet variables

Much of what a big bank's structure does — coordination, status transmission, brokering between layers — is exactly what AI does best. The exposed layer is the coordination middle. The question is a five-to-ten-year design problem, not a restructure announcement.

Crisis-led (the default)

Deny the question; run periodic redundancy rounds as pressure builds. Pay twice — payouts, then the trust destruction that stalls the next three years of adoption.

Attrition-led (by design)

10–15% natural turnover a year is enormous design space over five years. Model the target shape; hire, retrain and redeploy toward it. This path closes a little every year it isn't taken.

Redefine the manager role ahead of the structure: coach, quality owner, judgement-holder — the parts of management AI doesn't do.

Discussion

If an AI-native competitor could run our product set with a fifth of our headcount, what is our managed path — attrition-led by design, or crisis-led by default?

Part II · 19 — Build, buy, or partner

Partnerships deserve M&A-grade rigour, not procurement-grade

Build

Where it differentiates and rides proprietary data: workflow integration, eval harnesses, data products, patterns. Never build around a model weakness the market will fix.

Buy

Where the category is commoditising fast — let vendors racing each other fund the innovation; keep switching options open.

Partner

For frontier access and learning. The test is capability transfer: joint teams, residencies, co-built patterns. Tokens-for-dollars is a supply contract wearing a strategy costume.

Australian majors are already committing (CBA–OpenAI the most prominent). Overlay APRA's concentration expectations: abstraction, dual-sourcing, rehearsed exits — managed consciously at board level, not resolved by accumulating dependence.

Discussion

Does our biggest AI partnership have a capability-transfer scorecard — or is it a supply contract we're calling a strategy?

11 · Synthesis

The operating model is the moat

  • Models are commodities; metabolism is not.
  • Governance can be the accelerator — policy-as-code and real-time assurance let a regulated bank move faster, not slower.
  • Redesign journeys and functions, not tasks — customer service and finance are the proving grounds.
  • Work is re-platforming — the live operating layer is the substrate copilots and agents will run on.
  • People diverge unless the system converges them — the super-user / laggard split is an operating-model risk.
  • Talent and diffusion share a solution — the floating expert layer retains the best people and spreads new practice.
  • Benchmark globally, translate locally — Europe, US East Coast, US West Coast — each for a different question.
The question to leave in the room
How do we turn AI from a set of tools and pilots into an organisational capability — something that changes how risk, finance, customer service, talent, and work itself are designed?

Everyone will have the models. Advantage belongs to the organisation that metabolises them fastest.
